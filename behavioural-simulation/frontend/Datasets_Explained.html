◆ The Datasets — in plain English

To test our system, we invented 5 pretend investors, gave each a personality, and let them trade the real stock market for 6 years. That produced a handful of datasets. They look like intimidating financial spreadsheets — so here is what each one actually is, in everyday words, with a real row translated.

5 investors (a personality each)→ ① Receipt book→ ② Daily photo album→ ③ Decision diary→ ④ Recovered personalities→ ⑤ Satisfaction scores
1

The Receipt Book

5,954 trades
transaction_ledger.csv
Every single time an investor bought or sold, we wrote down a receipt: who, when, buy or sell, which company, the price, how many shares, and how much cash was left. It only records actions — if they did nothing that day, there's no receipt.
Think of it as: a bank statement of trades — one line for every purchase or sale, with a running cash balance, exactly like the transaction history in a banking app.
What it really looks like
WhoWhenActionCompanyPriceSharesCash left
INV_012020-01-07BUYCOMB63.962,345848,516
↓
On this day, Investor 1 bought 2,345 shares of a bank (Commercial Bank) at Rs. 63.96 each — spending about Rs. 150,000 and leaving Rs. 848,516 in cash.
2

The Daily Photo Album

7,915 daily snapshots
portfolio_snapshots.csv
At the end of every single day — whether they traded or not — we took a snapshot of each investor's finances: how much cash they held, how much their shares were worth, and the two added together (their total wealth).
Think of it as: checking your net worth every evening and writing it in a notebook — a photo of "what I own and what it's worth" for each day.
What it really looks like
WhenWhoCashShares worthTotal wealth
2020-01-01INV_011,000,00001,000,000
↓
On the first day, Investor 1 was holding Rs. 1,000,000 in cash and no shares yet, so their total wealth was Rs. 1,000,000. Every day after, this line shows how that wealth rose and fell.
3

The Decision Diary

7,915 decisions · the important one
behavioral_validation_log.csv
This is the one our system actually reads. For every day it records three things: what the investor did, how much their money changed since their last move, and whether they were ahead of or behind the market. Those last two are what reveal the personality.
Think of it as: a daily diary entry — "here's what I did, here's how much richer or poorer I got, and here's whether I beat everyone else or fell behind."
What it really looks like
WhoWhenDidMoney changeVs. the market
INV_012020-01-13SELL-3,359-0.15
↓
On this day Investor 1 chose to sell. Since their last move their money had gone down by Rs. 3,359, and they were doing very slightly better than the overall market. How someone reacts to being down, and to falling behind, is exactly what tells us their personality.
4

The Recovered Personalities

the answer-key check
recovered_parameters.csv
After reading the diary, our system reports the three personality numbers it dug out for each investor. Because we secretly set the true numbers ourselves, we can put them side by side and check.
Think of it as: marking an exam against the answer key — our system's guess next to the truth we already knew.
What it really looks like
WhoLoss aversionSensitivityRegret
INV_01 (recovered)2.010.910.36
INV_01 (the truth)2.250.880.50
↓
We had secretly made Investor 1 moderately loss-averse (2.25). Reading only their trades, the system reconstructed 2.01 — and the other two numbers match just as closely. The personality was recovered from behaviour alone.
5

The Satisfaction Scores

the final output
behavioral_utility.csv
Finally, using each investor's personality, we score every day with a single number: how satisfied that particular person would feel about what happened to their money that day. This score is what the portfolio system uses to build something the person can live with.
Think of it as: a daily mood rating for their money — positive means "happy with today," negative means "unhappy," and a very loss-averse person's bad days score far more negative than a calm person's.
What it really looks like
WhoWhenMoney changeVs. marketSatisfaction
INV_012020-01-030-0.02+0.01
↓
A quiet day — money barely moved and they slightly beat the market — so Investor 1's satisfaction is a tiny positive. On a day they lost a lot, this number would swing sharply negative, and more so the more loss-averse they are.

Every dataset here was produced by our five invented investors trading real market history. Together they let us do one thing: hide a known personality inside behaviour, and prove we can read it back out.
